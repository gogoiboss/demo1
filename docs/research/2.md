## 1. How single-line sections work: tokens, crossing stations, and precedence

Most of the world's single-line (non-double-track) railway operates on some form of **absolute block / token system**: only one train is allowed into a given block section at a time, in either direction, and a physical or electronic authority (a "token," "staff," or line-clear authority) proves this. This system is used because a single bidirectional track carries the risk of head-on collision if two trains entered simultaneously, so historically the simplest fix was to allow only one train in the section at a time ("one-engine-in-steam" or "one-train-working"), later extended to a token/ticket system where a driver could be shown the token without taking it, allowing a second train to follow the same direction.

On Indian Railways specifically, two operational terms matter for your model:

- **Crossing**: when two trains going in *opposite* directions need to use the same single line, they must "cross" at a station that has a loop line — one train is held in the loop while the other passes on the main line, then roles may reverse for the next single-line stretch.
- **Precedence**: when two trains going in the *same* direction (or contesting the same section, platform, or overtake) conflict, one is given right-of-way and the other is held back, regardless of which one is currently running late. The order of precedence for trains governs which train gets priority when two trains have to cross on a single line, or are waiting to use a platform at a station, and the train with higher precedence is given priority while the other is made to wait — normally regardless of how much detention this causes, even if the other train is already late. If two trains of the same precedence level are heading in opposite directions, the one nearer its destination is given priority, though crew duty-hour limits or fueling needs can override this.

Operationally, this is implemented through **station masters, section controllers, and control charts**: a control chart plots distance against time for each block section, colour-coded by train category (e.g., red for mail/express, blue for ordinary passenger, black for goods), and shows scheduled vs. actual paths, with annotations for late starts, crossings, and detentions. When a train has to be held back for precedence or crossing, formal signalling procedure requires the loco pilot to be notified in writing before the signal is put back to "on." There may also be scheduled "additional halts for crossings in single-line sections and precedence" that are baked into the working timetable itself, distinct from the public timetable — these are indicated only in the working timetable, not the passenger timetable.

## 2. How a delayed train blocks OTHER trains on the same section

This is the mechanism you actually need to encode. A few distinct propagation channels exist beyond "the train right behind it":

- **Section/block occupancy**: since only one train can hold the block authority at a time, a delayed train physically occupies the single line longer than scheduled, which delays *every* train scheduled to enter that block next — including opposite-direction trains waiting to cross at the next loop station, not just a same-direction follower.
- **Crossing-station knock-on**: if Train A is late reaching the crossing station, Train B (going the other way) sits in the loop longer, which can itself cascade if Train B was supposed to have made a tight connection or crossing further down its own route with Train C — this is exactly the "domino effect" formalized in delay-propagation literature. A single delayed train may cause a domino effect of secondary delays across an entire railway network, since propagation depends on the interplay between timetable structure and available buffer/recovery time — late trains use recovery time and slack (buffer times) between consecutive trains to absorb some of the delay before it spreads further.
- **Platform/loop-line contention at junctions**: a late train can occupy a platform or loop needed by an unrelated train, even one on a different route through the same station.
- **Not just adjacent trains**: disruptions in train timing are interconnected — a single out-of-schedule train can trigger a ripple effect that delays other trains and congests adjacent stations, reducing overall network efficiency well beyond the originally affected line.

A real, numbers-bearing India example of this exact mechanism: on the Bengaluru–Chennai single-line Hosur section, the Ernakulam Intercity Superfast Express was delayed by over 45 minutes, and the cascading effect resulted in trains on the Hosur line — including an unrelated local DEMU service — being delayed by about an hour, attributed by officials to congestion on the single line during morning peak hours when several trains arrive and depart on the mainline.

**Sources**: [Goverde, "A delay propagation algorithm for large-scale railway traffic networks," Transportation Research Part C, 2010](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0968090X10000124); [RSTGCN paper, arXiv:2510.01262](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01262); [Deccan Herald, Hosur rail roko report](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/india%2Fkarnataka%2Fbengaluru%2Fangry-passengers-stage-rail-705296.html).

## 3. Is there a formal priority/precedence order?

Yes — historically codified, informally updated over time.

The traditional formal order of precedence, from highest to lowest, was: breakdown trains headed to accident sites; the Presidential/Ministerial train; Mail Trains; Express Trains; Troop Trains; Specials engaged by the public; Ordinary Passenger trains; Mixed trains; Parcel trains; breakdown trains returning from accident sites; Fast through goods trains; Work trains; Ballast and Material trains. This reflected the historic importance of Mail Trains as the fastest category, but mail trains have since declined in relative importance, and modern classes like Rajdhanis and Shatabdis now outrank the old mail/express category.

Important nuances for your model — the real system is **not a strict fixed ladder**, it's regularly overridden by local operational judgment:

- Certain trains get precedence disproportionate to their formal schedule status because they are "Minister's list" or "board-monitored" trains — a politically determined designation typically covering each zone's prestige trains, the Rajdhanis and Shatabdis.
- Local passenger and EMU/MEMU trains serving many halts are often given *higher* precedence than long-distance mail/express and superfast trains (excepting Rajdhanis/Shatabdis), partly because they serve large numbers of local commuters and are directly under the local division's accountability, unlike through-trains whose punctuality responsibility is split across divisions. A concrete example: a local MEMU is scheduled to precede an Ahmedabad-bound express until a certain station, and even a Mumbai Rajdhani has been made to trail a local express on part of its route because the previous overtake arrangement caused too much delay to commuters.
- Goods trains can even be given precedence over passenger trains to improve track utilization when the goods train can run straight through while the passenger train has many halts — e.g., a goods train was scheduled to depart just five minutes ahead of a mail train because it could run clear through to the next major yard while the mail train needed over two hours for the same distance with six halts.
- Mail/superfast/express trains are generally still given priority over ordinary passenger trains, especially when the faster train is running late, and recovery/"catch-up" time is usually built into the final section of a train's run within a division to help it regain schedule.

Popularly cited modern informal priority lists (not an official IR document, treat as a rough heuristic, not ground truth for your model) put it roughly as: Vande Bharat, Rajdhani, Tejas, Duronto, Shatabdi, Gatiman, Garib Rath, Jan Shatabdi, Superfast Mail/Express, then ordinary Mail/Express. Treat this as informally sourced — for a hackathon model I'd encode it as a configurable rank rather than gospel, since real dispatchers override it constantly based on local commuter impact, crew hours, and control-office judgment.

**Sources**: [IRFCA Operations FAQ III](https://irfca.org/faq/faq-ops3.html) (most authoritative, community-maintained enthusiast reference widely used by rail researchers); [IRFCA Operations FAQ I](https://irfca.org/faq/faq-ops.html); popular-press summaries ([RailMitra](https://www.railmitra.com/blog/know-about-the-highest-priority-trains-of-indian-railways), [India TV](https://www.indiatvnews.com/web-stories/news/top-7-order-of-priority-of-track-allotment-among-trains-of-indian-railways-rajdhani-vande-bharat-express-shatabdi-express-2025-02-25-977952)) — useful for intuition, not authoritative.

## 4. Research on modeling delay propagation across a network

**Graph-based / deterministic (max-plus algebra, timed event graphs)** — this is the most directly buildable-in-a-week category:
- Goverde's max-plus algebra model represents the railway system as a linear system where the timetable is a timed event graph, computing delay propagation across both time and space via a "marked precedence graph," with structural delays (infeasible schedule) separated from propagated delays via a decomposition method. This paper is close to what you'd want to reimplement in miniature. [ScienceDirect link](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0968090X10000124)
- Other graph/network methods in this family include activity graphs (Büker and Seybold, 2012), Petri nets (Milinković et al., 2013), alternative graphs (Meloni et al., 2021), and Bayesian network models, with edge weights typically representing minimum running or dwelling process times.

**Stochastic graph models** (probability distributions on delay, not just point estimates):
- Büker and Seybold's activity-graph model represents each train-move status (entry-signal-reached, arrived, ready-to-depart, departing) as a node carrying a delay distribution (CDF), with edges representing the propagation relationships between these statuses across trains. [ScienceDirect link](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2210970612000182)
- Berger et al. (2011) propose a stochastic model predicting arrival/departure delays incorporating waiting policies, driving time profiles, and catch-up potential.

**Network-diffusion framing** (explicitly the language you used):
- A paper titled ["Modelling railway delay propagation as diffusion-like spreading"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.06111) exists on arXiv, framing delay spread using diffusion dynamics rather than discrete-event scheduling — worth reading if you want the "network diffusion" framing specifically, though I did not extract its results in depth here.
- A separate Scientific Reports paper proposes a graph-based, hybrid schedule-and-empirical model for delay propagation and applies spectral clustering to identify geographic regions of a network that behave as coherent delay "communities," tested on the Netherlands, Germany, Switzerland, and Italy. [Nature link](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-00361-z)

**Graph neural network approaches** (more ML-heavy, harder to build in a week, but directly on Indian data):
- The RSTGCN paper forecasts average arrival delays across Indian Railways stations using a graph convolutional network, motivated explicitly by the cascading nature of Indian delays — their September 2024 data analysis found an average arrival delay of about 51 minutes, with many instances exceeding 12 to 24 hours, and they released an open dataset. [arXiv:2510.01262](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01262) — this is probably your single best reference since it's India-specific, recent, and explicitly about cascading delay.
- The SAGE-Het model combines GraphSAGE with heterogeneous graph neural networks to capture interactions between trains and stations for network-wide delay evolution, tested on Chinese railway sub-networks.

**Queueing-theory framing**: less commonly used as a standalone full-network model in the literature I found (it appears more as a sub-component — e.g., "evaluating railway junction infrastructure: a queueing-based, timetable-independent analysis" is cited as one approach — used mainly for junction/station capacity analysis, not full network cascade propagation). If you want a queueing framing for a hackathon, the natural fit is modeling each shared block section as an M/M/1 or M/G/1 queue with trains as customers and "service time" as block occupancy time, but note this is a simplification, not the mainstream approach in the literature — most serious network-scale work uses the graph/max-plus/stochastic-graph family above instead.

**Indian-railway-specific delay studies** (mostly regression/ML on single-train delay, not full propagation, but useful for calibrating your model's parameters):
- Gaurav and Srivastava built an N-order Markov Late-Minutes Prediction Framework using two years of running-status data for 135 trains through Mughalsarai station — described as the first effort to predict train delays for the Indian rail network, complementing earlier structural work by Ghosh et al. [arXiv:1806.02825](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.02825)
- Ghosh et al.'s "Statistical analysis of the Indian Railway Network: A complex network approach" studies the IRN as a weighted complex network with stations as nodes and trains between them as weighted edges, finding small-world structure and exponential degree distribution, and that the network becomes denser and more homogeneous over time. This is useful background for justifying your graph topology choices.

## What's the minimum viable, technically-correct model for a week?

Given everything above, here's what I'd actually build, and why it's the right level of abstraction — it maps directly onto the **timed-event-graph / max-plus propagation family** (Goverde 2010; Büker & Seybold's simplified deterministic cousin), which is the most established and also the simplest to implement correctly:

**Core idea**: model the railway as a directed graph of *events* (a train arriving at or departing a station/block boundary), not trains as monolithic objects. Delay propagates forward through this graph via one recursive rule.

1. **Data model**: for each train, its scheduled sequence of (station, arrival_time, departure_time) events. For each pair of trains sharing a block/section, encode the *conflict edges*: which train has precedence when both want the same resource at overlapping times.
2. **Two edge types** (this is the whole trick):
   - *Running-time edges* (same train): `next_event_time ≥ prev_event_time + min_running_time`.
   - *Resource-conflict edges* (different trains, shared block or platform): `following_train_event_time ≥ preceding_train_event_time + min_headway`, where "preceding" is decided by your precedence rules (category rank, or "who's there first" for same-rank).
3. **Propagation rule** (this *is* max-plus algebra, just don't call it that if it intimidates the team): for every event, actual_time = max(scheduled_time, max over all incoming edges of (source_actual_time + edge_weight)). Process events in topological order (by scheduled time) and this converges in one pass — no simulation loop needed.
4. **Precedence resolution**: when two trains contend for the same block at overlapping times, decide which one's event gets to be the "source" of the conflict edge using your rank table from Q3 (configurable, not hardcoded — this is your project's actual contribution). This is where you can be honest that real IR precedence has exceptions (Q3) and make that a tunable/explainable parameter rather than pretending it's deterministic.
5. **Output**: for a given train, its delay at each downstream station is just its computed actual_time minus scheduled_time — and because it's one graph, you get *network-wide* propagation (including the "not directly following" case from Q2) for free, since any train's event can sit downstream of any other train's event via a resource-conflict edge, not just a same-line-following edge.

This is buildable in a week because: it's a single forward graph traversal (no optimization, no stochastic sampling needed for a v1), the hard modeling work is just correctly identifying conflict edges from your schedule/section data, and it is *not* hand-waved — it is a simplified, deterministic instance of exactly the timed-event-graph approach used in the peer-reviewed literature above. A stretch goal, if time remains, is swapping point-estimates for simple triangular/exponential delay distributions per edge (a mini version of Büker & Seybold) to get confidence intervals instead of single numbers — but the deterministic version alone is a legitimate, citable modeling choice, not a toy.
